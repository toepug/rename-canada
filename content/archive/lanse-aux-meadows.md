The asset is a Norse encampment on the northern tip of Newfoundland, built from timber felled in the year 1021 CE — predating the Confederation that created the designation "Canada" by 846 years. It is the oldest asset in this Program's current inventory by a margin of centuries. This finding, and a numbering discrepancy with a related file, are discussed in 3.1.

## 2.0 Historical record

*Documented per TB Reg. 2024-117, s. 12(b).*

### 2.1 A saga, and a guide (to 1960)

Icelandic sagas described Norse voyages, around the year 1000, to a western land they called Vinland — treated for centuries as legend rather than history. Norwegian explorer **Helge Ingstad**, convinced the accounts described a real landfall, searched the coasts of northeastern North America through the 1950s. In 1960, local resident **George Decker** led him to a set of overgrown mounds near the village of L'Anse aux Meadows, long known locally only as the "old Indian camp."

### 2.2 Eight years, eight buildings (1961–1968)

Ingstad's wife, archaeologist **Anne Stine Ingstad**, led international excavations at the site from 1961 to 1968. The team uncovered eight timber-framed, sod-covered buildings built in an Icelandic and early-Greenlandic style, along with cooking pits and an iron forge — the only confirmed evidence of iron production anywhere in the Americas before the era of sustained European contact centuries later. Among roughly 800 recovered artifacts were a bronze cloak-fastening pin, a bone needle, and a soapstone spindle whorl.

### 2.3 A date, precisely (2021)

For decades the site could be dated only approximately, to "around 1000 CE," the limit of what building style and radiocarbon analysis could offer. A 2021 study published in *Nature* used a different method. A global cosmic-ray event in 993 CE had left a detectable signature in that year's tree-ring growth worldwide; researchers located that ring in wood cut for the site's buildings, then counted outward to the bark. The wood had been felled in the year 1021 CE — the first time any Norse activity in the Americas has been dated to an exact year.

### 2.4 Recognition (1968–1978)

The site was designated a National Historic Site of Canada in November 1968, before excavation had concluded, and inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in September 1978, one of the original twelve sites on the very first World Heritage List. Canada placed two sites on that inaugural list — this one, and Nahanni National Park.

## 3.0 Deprecation assessment

### 3.1 Naming and provenance exposure

The asset predates the Confederation that created the designation "Canada" by 846 years, calculated from the 2021 tree-ring date of 1021 CE. The Secretariat notes that a related file in this Archive's Rejected Designations Annex (A.4, Vinland) cites a predating interval of 867 years, calculated from the earlier and less precise estimate of "circa 1000 CE." The two figures have not been reconciled, and the Secretariat declines to determine which file is in error, both having been prepared to the same standard of care.

### 3.2 Jurisdictional constraints (self-defeating finding)

This is, by a margin of centuries, the oldest asset in the Program's current inventory. It is jointly protected by a 1968 federal historic-site designation and a 1978 UNESCO World Heritage inscription — one of only two Canadian sites in UNESCO's original cohort. No comparable authority is available to the Secretariat, and no comparable age has been recorded elsewhere in this Archive.

### 3.3 Disposition

Sunset is warranted on the merits and cannot presently be executed. The asset has been placed in the **deferred queue**, where it will be reviewed quarterly, indefinitely, under whichever date proves correct. The Secretariat is satisfied that this constitutes progress.
