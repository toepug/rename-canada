The asset is a wartime harbour collision between two cargo ships, one carrying a full load of military explosives, that detonated in the middle of a populated city. It is the largest human-made explosion in history prior to the nuclear age, and it killed close to two thousand people in under a tenth of a second.

## 2.0 Historical record

*Documented per TB Reg. 2024-117, s. 12(b).*

### 2.1 The collision (6 December 1917, morning)

At approximately 8:45 a.m. on 6 December 1917, the French cargo ship *Mont-Blanc* — inbound to join a transatlantic munitions convoy, loaded with a full wartime cargo of picric acid, TNT, and guncotton, with drums of benzol fuel lashed across her open deck — collided with the Norwegian-registered *Imo*, outbound on a chartered Belgian war-relief run, in the Narrows, the channel connecting Halifax's Bedford Basin to its harbour. The collision sparked a fire on *Mont-Blanc*'s deck.

### 2.2 Twenty minutes (8:45–9:04 a.m.)

Unable to fight the fire and aware of what their ship carried, *Mont-Blanc*'s crew abandoned her and rowed for the Dartmouth shore, shouting warnings that were not understood or believed. The abandoned, burning ship drifted against Pier 6, in the densely populated Richmond district of Halifax's North End. Crowds gathered at the waterfront and in windows overlooking the harbour, including many schoolchildren, to watch the spectacle, unaware of the danger it posed. At the Richmond Street rail yard, dispatcher **Vince Coleman** remained at his telegraph key after being warned to flee, sending word up the line to stop an incoming passenger train: "Hold up the train. Ammunition ship afire in harbour making for Pier 6 and will explode. Guess this will be my last message. Good-bye, boys." The train stopped safely at Rockingham, more than six kilometres away. Coleman did not survive.

### 2.3 The explosion (9:04 a.m.)

*Mont-Blanc* exploded with a force estimated at roughly 2.9 kilotons — the largest human-made explosion in history prior to the nuclear age. The blast levelled some 325 acres of Richmond and the adjoining districts, threw a wave onto the Dartmouth shore, and was felt more than a hundred kilometres away. At least 1,782 people are confirmed to have died in the explosion or its immediate aftermath; commonly cited estimates of the total dead, including people who died later of their injuries and remains never identified, run as high as approximately 2,000. An estimated 9,000 more were injured. Several hundred people — many of them children who had been watching from windows when the blast drove glass inward — lost some or all of their sight; published figures for the exact number vary, from the low hundreds to as many as 850 [VERIFY: figure varies by source], but the event is consistently described as the largest mass-blinding incident in Canadian history.

### 2.4 Aftermath (December 1917 – 1985)

A blizzard struck Halifax the following morning, burying the ruins under some sixteen inches of snow and hampering a rescue effort already underway in darkness and freezing cold. Relief arrived from across Canada and beyond; a Massachusetts relief train, organized within hours of the news reaching Boston, departed before the blizzard had cleared and reached Halifax still in the storm. In gratitude, the province of Nova Scotia sent Boston a Christmas tree in 1918, and again, starting in 1971, every year since. The Canadian National Institute for the Blind was incorporated on 30 March 1918, founded in direct response to the number of Halifax residents left permanently blind. A government inquiry found *Mont-Blanc*'s pilot, her captain, and the naval officer in charge of the harbour's wartime defences at fault; manslaughter charges against the first two were dropped for lack of evidence, and the only man tried, the naval officer, was acquitted. Courts later held the two ships equally responsible for the collision. No one was ever convicted of a crime arising from it. A carillon of bells intended decades earlier for a Halifax church was instead installed as the Halifax Explosion Memorial Bell Tower, overlooking the Narrows from Fort Needham, and dedicated on 9 June 1985.
